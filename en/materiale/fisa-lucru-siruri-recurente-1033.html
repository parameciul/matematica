The question of this worksheet: How do you find the 100th term of a sequence given by a recurrence directly, without computing all the terms before it, and how do you study whether it is monotonic and bounded?

Start from what you know

Three quick checks. If one of them gives you trouble, write it down: go back to it first.

  1. Fill in: if $(a_n)$ is an arithmetic progression with $a_1=4$ and common difference $r=3$, then $a_n=\ldots$; if $(b_n)$ is a geometric progression with $b_1=2$ and common ratio $q=5$, then $b_n=\ldots$

  2. Compute $1+2+\ldots+20$ and $1+2+2^2+\ldots+2^9$. Which formulas did you use?

  3. Solve the equation $r^2-2r+4=0$ in $\mathbb{C}$ and write the solutions in trigonometric form.

Short theory

For each type of recurrence: how to recognise it, the method step by step (in the thick frame) and worked examples. At the end you find the summary table and the logical thread.

1. The linear recurrence of order 1

Definition

A relation of the form $x_{n+1}=a\cdot x_n+b$, with real numbers $a, b$, is called a linear recurrence of order 1. If $a=1$, the sequence is an arithmetic progression. If $b=0$, the sequence is a geometric progression.

★ Method

The fixed-point method ($a\ne 1$)

  1. Find the fixed point: put $\ell$ in place of $x_{n+1}$ and of $x_n$. From $\ell=a\ell+b$ you get $\ell=\frac{b}{1-a}$.
  2. Subtract the two relations: $x_{n+1}-\ell=a(x_n-\ell)$. The sequence $y_n=x_n-\ell$ is a geometric progression with ratio $a$.
  3. Write $y_n=y_1\cdot a^{n-1}$.
  4. Go back to $x_n=y_n+\ell$ and check it on $x_2$.

Remember

$$x_n=\ell+(x_1-\ell)\cdot a^{n-1}$$

If the sequence starts with $x_0$, the exponent becomes $n$: $x_n=\ell+(x_0-\ell)\cdot a^n$.

Example 1 · Step by step

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=5$, $x_{n+1}=3x_n-4$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. $\ell=3\ell-4\Rightarrow \ell=2$.

Step 2. $y_n=x_n-2$ satisfies $y_{n+1}=3y_n$, and $y_1=5-2=3$.

Step 3. $y_n=3\cdot 3^{n-1}=3^n$.

Step 4. $x_n=3^n+2$. Check: from the relation $x_2=11$; from the formula $9+2=11$ ✓.

Example 2 · With fractions

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=1$, $x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}x_n+3$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. $\ell=\frac{1}{2}\ell+3\Rightarrow \ell=6$.

Steps 2–3. $y_n=x_n-6$, $y_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}y_n$, $y_1=-5$, so $y_n=-\frac{5}{2^{n-1}}$.

Step 4. $x_n=6-\frac{5}{2^{n-1}}$. Check: $x_2=\frac{1}{2}+3=\frac{7}{2}$ and $6-\frac{5}{2}=\frac{7}{2}$ ✓.

2. The homogeneous linear recurrence of order 2

Definition

A relation of the form $x_{n+2}=a\cdot x_{n+1}+b\cdot x_n$ is called a homogeneous linear recurrence of order 2. Each term comes from the two terms before it.

★ Method

The characteristic equation method

  1. Write the characteristic equation: replace $x_{n+2}$ with $r^2$, $x_{n+1}$ with $r$ and $x_n$ with 1. You get $r^2=ar+b$.
  2. Compute $\Delta$ and the roots. Write the form of $x_n$ from the table below.
  3. Find $A$ and $B$ from the first two terms: replace $n$ with their indices and solve the system.
  4. Check the formula on the third term, computed from the relation.
Δ The roots Form of the general term
$\Delta>0$ two real roots $r_1\ne r_2$ $x_n=A\cdot r_1^n+B\cdot r_2^n$
$\Delta=0$ one double root $r$ $x_n=(A+B\cdot n)\cdot r^n$
$\Delta<0$ $r_{1,2}=\rho(\cos{t}\pm i\sin{t})$ $x_n=\rho^n\cdot (A\cos{nt}+B\sin{nt})$

Example 3 · Δ > 0

Find the general term of the sequence $x_0=2$, $x_1=7$, $x_{n+2}=5x_{n+1}-6x_n$, $n\in \mathbb{N}$.

Steps 1–2. $r^2-5r+6=0$, $\Delta=1>0$, $r_1=2$, $r_2=3$. So $x_n=A\cdot 2^n+B\cdot 3^n$.

Step 3. $n=0$: $A+B=2$; $n=1$: $2A+3B=7$. You get $B=3$, $A=-1$, so $x_n=3^{n+1}-2^n$.

Step 4. From the relation: $x_2=5\cdot 7-6\cdot 2=23$. From the formula: $27-4=23$ ✓.

Example 4 · Δ = 0

Find the general term of the sequence $x_0=2$, $x_1=4$, $x_{n+2}=8x_{n+1}-16x_n$, $n\in \mathbb{N}$.

Steps 1–2. $r^2-8r+16=0$, $\Delta=0$, $r=4$ (double root). So $x_n=(A+B\cdot n)\cdot 4^n$.

Step 3. $n=0$: $A=2$; $n=1$: $(A+B)\cdot 4=4$, so $A+B=1$ and $B=-1$. You get $x_n=(2-n)\cdot 4^n$.

Step 4. From the relation: $x_2=8\cdot 4-16\cdot 2=0$. From the formula: $0\cdot 16=0$ ✓.

Example 5 · Δ < 0

Find the general term of the sequence $x_0=1$, $x_1=2$, $x_{n+2}=-4x_n$, $n\in \mathbb{N}$.

Steps 1–2. $r^2+4=0$, $\Delta=-16<0$, $r=\pm 2i=2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\pm i\sin\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, so $\rho=2$, $t=\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $x_n=2^n\cdot \left(A\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}+B\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)$.

Step 3. $n=0$: $A=1$; $n=1$: $2\cdot (A\cdot 0+B\cdot 1)=2$, so $B=1$.

$$x_n=2^n\cdot \left(\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}+\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)$$

Step 4. From the relation: $x_2=-4\cdot 1=-4$. From the formula: $4\cdot (-1+0)=-4$ ✓.

Watch out · The case Δ = 0

For $\Delta=0$ do not write $A\cdot 4^n+B\cdot 4^n$. This is really $(A+B)\cdot 4^n$, a single constant, and the system for $A$ and $B$ has, in general, no solution. That is why the factor $n$ appears.

Remember · A trick for Δ < 0

From Vieta’s formulas: $\rho=\sqrt{-b}$ and $\cos{t}=\frac{a}{2\rho}$, with $t\in (0;\pi)$. If $\rho=1$, the sequence repeats itself (it is periodic).

3. Additive, multiplicative and mixed recurrences

Definitions

  • Additive recurrence: $x_{n+1}=x_n+f(n)$, where $f(n)$ depends only on $n$.
  • Multiplicative recurrence: $x_{n+1}=g(n)\cdot x_n$.
  • Mixed recurrence: $x_{n+1}=a\cdot x_n+(\text{something with }a^n)$.

★ Method

The telescoping method

  1. Write the relation for $1; 2; \ldots; n-1$, one below the other.
  2. Additive: add the equalities. Multiplicative: multiply them. Mixed: first divide by $a^{n+1}$; the sequence $y_n=\frac{x_n}{a^n}$ satisfies an additive recurrence.
  3. The terms in the middle cancel. Compute the sum (or the product) that is left.
  4. Check the formula on $x_2$.

Remember

$$x_n=x_1+f(1)+f(2)+\ldots+f(n-1)$$

$$x_n=x_1\cdot g(1)\cdot g(2)\cdot \ldots\cdot g(n-1)$$

Useful sums:

$$1+2+\ldots+m=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}$$

$$1+3+\ldots+(2m-1)=m^2$$

$$1+q+\ldots+q^{m-1}=\frac{q^m-1}{q-1}\quad (q\ne 1)$$

Example 6 · Additive recurrence

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=3$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+2n+1$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. $x_2=x_1+3$, $x_3=x_2+5$, …, $x_n=x_{n-1}+(2n-1)$.

Step 2. Add: $x_n=3+(3+5+\ldots+(2n-1))=3+(n^2-1)$, so $x_n=n^2+2$.

Check: $x_2=3+3=6$ and $2^2+2=6$ ✓.

Example 7 · Multiplicative recurrence

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=2$, $x_{n+1}=\frac{n+2}{n}\cdot x_n$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. Multiply the relations for $1; 2; \ldots; n-1$:

$$x_n=2\cdot \frac{3}{1}\cdot \frac{4}{2}\cdot \frac{5}{3}\cdot \ldots\cdot \frac{n}{n-2}\cdot \frac{n+1}{n-1}$$

Step 2. The factors cancel “diagonally”: $n$ and $n+1$ stay on top, 1 and 2 at the bottom. So $x_n=2\cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2}=n(n+1)$.

Check: $x_2=3\cdot 2=6$ and $2\cdot 3=6$ ✓.

Example 8 · Mixed recurrence

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=1$, $x_{n+1}=2x_n+2^n$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. Divide by $2^{n+1}$: $\frac{x_{n+1}}{2^{n+1}}=\frac{x_n}{2^n}+\frac{1}{2}$.

Step 2. $y_n=\frac{x_n}{2^n}$ is an arithmetic progression with common difference $\frac{1}{2}$ and $y_1=\frac{1}{2}$, so $y_n=\frac{n}{2}$.

Step 3. $x_n=2^n\cdot \frac{n}{2}=n\cdot 2^{n-1}$. Check: $x_2=2+2=4$ and $2\cdot 2=4$ ✓.

Watch out · How many terms does the sum have?

You write the relation for $1; 2; \ldots; n-1$, so the sum (or the product) has $n-1$ terms, not $n$. That is why $m=n-1$ appears in the formulas above.

4. Recurrences with radicals

Three tricks

Trick 1 · Square it. If $\sqrt{\ldots x_n^2\ldots}$ appears, let $y_n=x_n^2$.

Trick 2 · The hidden perfect square. $x_n\pm 2\sqrt{x_n}+1=\left(\sqrt{x_n}\pm 1\right)^2$; let $y_n=\sqrt{x_n}$.

Trick 3 · Take logarithms. If the relation has only products, powers and radicals (for example $x_{n+1}=c\cdot \sqrt{x_n}$), let $y_n=\log_c{x_n}$. Powers become products, and products become sums.

★ Method

The substitution method

  1. Recognise the right trick: square it, look for the hidden perfect square or take logarithms.
  2. Substitute: $y_n=x_n^2$, $y_n=\sqrt{x_n}$ or $y_n=\log_c{x_n}$.
  3. Solve the recurrence for $y_n$: usually it is a progression or a recurrence of type 1.
  4. Go back to $x_n$, after you check the sign of the terms.

Example 9 · The hidden perfect square

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=4$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+2\sqrt{x_n}+1$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. Recognise $a^2+2a+1=(a+1)^2$ with $a=\sqrt{x_n}$: $x_{n+1}=\left(\sqrt{x_n}+1\right)^2$.

Step 2. The terms are positive, so $\sqrt{x_{n+1}}=\sqrt{x_n}+1$. The sequence $y_n=\sqrt{x_n}$ is an arithmetic progression, with $y_1=2$.

Step 3. $y_n=n+1$, so $x_n=(n+1)^2$. Check: $x_2=4+4+1=9$ and $3^2=9$ ✓.

Example 10 · Taking logarithms

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=16$, $x_{n+1}=2\sqrt{x_n}$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. Take logarithms in base 2: $\log_2{x_{n+1}}=1+\frac{1}{2}\log_2{x_n}$.

Step 2. $y_n=\log_2{x_n}$ satisfies $y_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}y_n+1$, with $y_1=4$: a recurrence of type 1!

Step 3. $\ell=2$, so $y_n=2+2\cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}=2+\frac{1}{2^{n-2}}$ and $x_n=2^{2+\frac{1}{2^{n-2}}}$.

Check: $x_2=2\cdot 4=8$ and $2^{2+1}=8$ ✓.

Watch out · The sign

From $x_{n+1}^2=\ldots$ you get $x_{n+1}=\sqrt{\ldots}$ only if you know that the terms are positive. Check the sign first!

5. Polynomial recurrences

Two situations

A. $x_{n+1}$ is a polynomial in $x_n$. Look for a constant $c$ with $x_{n+1}+c=(x_n+c)^k$. For degree 2, the coefficient of $x_n$ must be $2c$.

B. $x_{n+1}=a\cdot x_n+P(n)$, with $P$ a polynomial in $n$ and $a\ne 1$. The same idea as the fixed point: look for a polynomial $p_n$, of the same degree as $P$, that satisfies the relation.

★ Method

The substitution method

  1. Look for what “copies” the relation: the constant $c$ (situation A) or the polynomial $p_n$ (situation B).
  2. Let $y_n=x_n+c$ (A), or $y_n=x_n-p_n$ (B).
  3. A: $y_{n+1}=y_n^k$, so $y_n=y_1^{k^{n-1}}$. B: $y_{n+1}=a\cdot y_n$, so $y_n=y_1\cdot a^{n-1}$.
  4. Go back to $x_n$ and check it on the first terms.

Remember · How to recognise it

“$x_{n+1}=x_n^2+2x_n$” → “$x_{n+1}+1$ is the square of $x_n+1$” → $y_{n+1}=y_n^2$.

If you see $2x_n^2-1$, think of $\cos{2t}=2\cos^2{t}-1$: “the angle doubles”. If you see $4x_n^3-3x_n$, think of $\cos{3t}=4\cos^3{t}-3\cos{t}$.

Example 11 · The cosine substitution

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=\frac{1}{2}$, $x_{n+1}=2x_n^2-1$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. Write $x_1=\cos\frac{\pi}{3}$. Then $x_2=2\cos^2\frac{\pi}{3}-1=\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}$.

Step 2. At each step the angle doubles. By induction: $x_n=\cos\frac{2^{n-1}\pi}{3}$.

Check: $x_2=2\cdot \frac{1}{4}-1=-\frac{1}{2}=\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}$ ✓.

Example 12 · A polynomial term in n

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=1$, $x_{n+1}=2x_n+n$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. Look for $p_n=\alpha n+\beta$ with $\alpha (n+1)+\beta=2(\alpha n+\beta)+n$.

Step 2. Compare coefficients: for $n$, $\alpha=2\alpha+1\Rightarrow \alpha=-1$; constant terms: $\alpha+\beta=2\beta\Rightarrow \beta=-1$.

Step 3. $y_n=x_n+n+1$ satisfies $y_{n+1}=2y_n$, $y_1=3$, so $y_n=3\cdot 2^{n-1}$.

Step 4. $x_n=3\cdot 2^{n-1}-n-1$. Check: $x_2=3$ and $6-3=3$ ✓; $x_3=8$ and $12-4=8$ ✓.

6. The homographic recurrence

Definition

A sequence given by a relation of the form

$$x_{n+1}=\frac{a\cdot x_n+b}{c\cdot x_n+d}$$

is called a sequence given by a homographic recurrence. The solutions of the equation $x=\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ are called the fixed points of the recurrence.

★ Method

The method of fixed points

  1. Solve the fixed-point equation $x=\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}$. You get a quadratic equation.
  2. Choose the substitution by the number of solutions (table below).
  3. Write $y_n$ (or $z_n$) with the formula of the progression.
  4. Go back to $x_n$ and check it on $x_2$.
Fixed points Substitution What you get
two: $\alpha\ne \beta$ $y_n=\frac{x_n-\alpha}{x_n-\beta}$ $(y_n)$ is a geometric progression
one: $\alpha$ $z_n=\frac{1}{x_n-\alpha}$ $(z_n)$ is an arithmetic progression
none ($\Delta<0$) – look for the period: usually the terms repeat

Remember · How to find the ratio

Subtract the fixed point from the relation. For any fixed point $\alpha$:

$$x_{n+1}-\alpha=\frac{(a-c\alpha)(x_n-\alpha)}{c\cdot x_n+d}$$

For two fixed points, divide the two equalities: the denominator $c\cdot x_n+d$ cancels.

Remember · If there are no fixed points

Usually the sequence repeats itself. Compute terms until $x_1$ comes back. If the terms repeat every $p$ terms, divide the index by $p$ and look at the remainder.

Example 13 · Two fixed points

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=3$, $x_{n+1}=\frac{4x_n-2}{x_n+1}$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. $x=\frac{4x-2}{x+1}\Rightarrow x^2-3x+2=0\Rightarrow \alpha=1$, $\beta=2$.

Step 2. Subtract the fixed points from the relation:

$$x_{n+1}-1=\frac{3(x_n-1)}{x_n+1},\quad x_{n+1}-2=\frac{2(x_n-2)}{x_n+1}$$

Divide: $y_{n+1}=\frac{3}{2}\cdot y_n$, where $y_n=\frac{x_n-1}{x_n-2}$.

Step 3. $y_1=\frac{3-1}{3-2}=2$, so $y_n=2\cdot \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n-1}$.

Step 4. From $y_n(x_n-2)=x_n-1$ you get $x_n=\frac{2y_n-1}{y_n-1}=\frac{8\cdot 3^{n-1}-2^n}{4\cdot 3^{n-1}-2^n}$. Check: $x_2=\frac{10}{4}=\frac{5}{2}$ and $\frac{24-4}{12-4}=\frac{5}{2}$ ✓.

Example 14 · A single fixed point

Find the general term of the sequence $x_1=0$, $x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2-x_n}$, $n\ge 1$.

Step 1. $x(2-x)=1\Rightarrow x^2-2x+1=0\Rightarrow \alpha=1$ (a single solution).

Step 2. $x_{n+1}-1=\frac{x_n-1}{2-x_n}$. Take the reciprocal:

$$\frac{1}{x_{n+1}-1}=\frac{2-x_n}{x_n-1}=\frac{1}{x_n-1}-1$$

So $z_{n+1}=z_n-1$, where $z_n=\frac{1}{x_n-1}$.

Step 3. $z_1=-1$, so $z_n=-n$.

Step 4. $x_n=1+\frac{1}{z_n}=1-\frac{1}{n}$. Check: $x_2=\frac{1}{2}$ and $1-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}$ ✓.

Example 15 · No fixed points (a periodic sequence)

Let $x_1=1$, $x_{n+1}=-\frac{1}{x_n+1}$, $n\ge 1$. We compute $x_{2026}$.

Step 1. $x=-\frac{1}{x+1}\Rightarrow x^2+x+1=0$, $\Delta=-3<0$: there are no fixed points.

Step 2. Compute the terms: $x_2=-\frac{1}{2}$, $x_3=-2$, $x_4=-\frac{1}{-2+1}=1=x_1$. The terms repeat every 3 terms: $1; -\frac{1}{2}; -2; 1; -\frac{1}{2}; -2; \ldots$

Step 3. $2026=3\cdot 675+1$. The remainder is 1, so $x_{2026}=x_1=1$.

7. Monotonicity and boundedness of a sequence given by a recurrence

Recall

The sequence $(x_n)$ is increasing if $x_n\le x_{n+1}$ and strictly increasing if $x_n<x_{n+1}$, for every $n$. The same goes for decreasing and strictly decreasing.

The sequence $(x_n)$ is bounded if there are numbers $m$ and $M$ such that $m\le x_n\le M$, for every $n$.

★ Method

Method · Monotonicity and boundedness

  1. Compute the first terms and make a guess: does it grow? does it drop? between which values does it stay?
  2. If you know the general term, study the sign of the difference $x_{n+1}-x_n$ directly and find the bounds from the formula.
  3. If you do not know the general term, prove a bound by induction. For a recurrence with a fixed point $\alpha$, the sign of $x_{n+1}-\alpha$ shows on which side of $\alpha$ the sequence stays.
  4. Study the sign of $x_{n+1}-x_n$ using the relation and the bound you found.
  5. Write the conclusion: the sequence is (strictly) increasing or decreasing and $m\le x_n\le M$.

Remember

  • An increasing sequence is bounded below by its first term. A decreasing sequence is bounded above by its first term. Only one bound is left to find.
  • For a sequence with positive terms you can also compare the ratio $\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}$ with 1.
  • If $x_{n+1}-\alpha=k\cdot (x_n-\alpha)$ with $k>0$, all the terms are on the same side of $\alpha$ as $x_1$ (by induction).

Example 16 · From the general term

We study the monotonicity and boundedness of the sequence from Example 14, $x_n=1-\frac{1}{n}$, $n\ge 1$.

Monotonicity: $x_{n+1}-x_n=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1}=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}>0$, so the sequence is strictly increasing.

Boundedness: the sequence is increasing, so $x_n\ge x_1=0$. Also $x_n=1-\frac{1}{n}<1$. So $0\le x_n<1$: the sequence is bounded.

Example 17 · Directly from the recurrence

Let $x_1=6$, $x_{n+1}=\frac{5x_n+4}{x_n+2}$, $n\ge 1$. We show that the sequence is monotonic and bounded.

Fixed points: $x^2+2x=5x+4\Rightarrow x^2-3x-4=0$, so $\alpha=4$ and $\beta=-1$.

Boundedness: $x_{n+1}-4=\frac{x_n-4}{x_n+2}$. If $x_n>4$, then $x_{n+1}>4$. Since $x_1=6>4$, by induction $x_n>4$ for every $n$.

Monotonicity: $x_{n+1}-x_n=\frac{5x_n+4-x_n^2-2x_n}{x_n+2}=-\frac{(x_n-4)(x_n+1)}{x_n+2}<0$, so the sequence is strictly decreasing.

Conclusion: the sequence is decreasing, so $x_n\le x_1=6$. Therefore $4<x_n\le 6$: the sequence is bounded.

Watch out · Sequences that are not monotonic or not bounded

To show that a sequence is not monotonic, three terms are enough, for example $x_0<x_1$ and $x_1>x_2$. To show that it is not bounded, show that its terms exceed any number.

Example: the sequence from Example 5, $x_n=2^n\cdot \left(\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}+\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)$. We have $x_0=1<x_1=2$ and $x_1>x_2=-4$, so the sequence is not monotonic. For $n=4k$: $x_{4k}=16^k$, and $16^k$ exceeds any number, so the sequence is not bounded.

Summary table: how to recognise the type of recurrence

Type Form of the relation What you do
1 · Linear of order 1 $x_{n+1}=a x_n+b$ the fixed point $\ell=a\ell+b$; then $x_n=\ell+(x_1-\ell)a^{n-1}$
2 · Linear of order 2 $x_{n+2}=a x_{n+1}+b x_n$ the characteristic equation $r^2=ar+b$; the form by $\Delta$; for $\Delta=0$ do not forget the factor $n$!
3 · Additive, multiplicative, mixed $x_{n+1}=x_n+f(n)$; $x_{n+1}=g(n)x_n$; $x_{n+1}=ax_n+(\text{something with }a^n)$ add the relations; multiply the relations; divide by $a^{n+1}$
4 · With radicals $\sqrt{\ldots}$ in the relation square it, look for $\left(\sqrt{x_n}\pm 1\right)^2$ or take logarithms; check the sign
5 · Polynomial $x_{n+1}=P(x_n)$ or $x_{n+1}=ax_n+P(n)$ $x_{n+1}+c=(x_n+c)^k$; $2x^2-1$ → cosine; the polynomial $p_n$
6 · Homographic $x_{n+1}=\frac{ax_n+b}{cx_n+d}$ the fixed points; $y_n=\frac{x_n-\alpha}{x_n-\beta}$ or $z_n=\frac{1}{x_n-\alpha}$; no fixed points → the period

Remember · The common idea

For types 1, 5 and 6 you look for what stays unchanged: the fixed point $\ell$, the polynomial $p_n$ or the fixed points. Subtracting them from the relation gives a progression.

★ Most important

The logical thread: what to do when you meet a recurrence. Follow the steps in this order, in every problem

  1. Compute the first 3–4 terms.
  2. Recognise the form of the relation (type 1–6).
  3. Choose the method: the fixed point (or the fixed points), Δ, telescoping, a substitution.
  4. Find the constants from the first terms.
  5. Check the formula on one more term.
  6. Study monotonicity and boundedness: from the formula or directly from the relation.

Problems on three levels

In each problem write, besides the answer, which type of recurrence you recognised and how. Check every formula on the first terms.

Basic level

  1. Classify each recurrence (type 1–6 or a progression) and write the first step of the method:

    a) $x_{n+1}=x_n+n^2$;

Recurrences in real life

The situations below are made up, but they resemble the ones you meet around you. You translate a story into a recurrence relation and use it to predict what comes next.

A. Savings. You put 1000 lei into an account with 5% interest per year and, at the end of each year, you add 200 lei more. Let $s_n$ be the amount after $n$ years ($s_0=1000$).

B. Medicine. A patient takes 100 mg of a medicine every morning. In 24 hours, the body removes half of the amount present. Let $m_n$ be the amount right after the $n$-th dose.

C. Fish in a lake. There are 1000 fish in a lake. Every year the population grows by 20%, then 300 fish are caught. Let $p_n$ be the number of fish after $n$ years ($p_0=1000$).

D. The Towers of Hanoi. To move a tower of $n+1$ discs, you move the tower on top ($n$ discs), then the big disc, then the tower of $n$ discs again. Let $h_n$ be the number of moves for $n$ discs ($h_1=1$).

For each situation

1. Write the recurrence relation and the first term.

2. Recognise the type and find the general term.

3. Check the formula on the first two terms, computed directly from the story.

4. Study the monotonicity and boundedness of the sequence. (B: show that $m_n<200$ for every $n$. C: in which year do the fish run out?)

Frequently asked questions

How do I find the general term of a sequence given by a linear recurrence of order 2?

Write the characteristic equation: replace xₙ₊₂ with r², xₙ₊₁ with r and xₙ with 1. The sign of Δ gives the form of the general term: A·r₁ⁿ + B·r₂ⁿ for two different real roots, (A + B·n)·rⁿ for a double root and ρⁿ(A cos nt + B sin nt) for complex roots. You find the constants A and B from the first two terms.

What is the homographic recurrence and how do you solve it?

It is a relation of the form xₙ₊₁ = (a·xₙ + b)/(c·xₙ + d). Solve the fixed-point equation x = (ax + b)/(cx + d). For two fixed points α and β, the sequence (xₙ − α)/(xₙ − β) is a geometric progression. For a single fixed point α, the sequence 1/(xₙ − α) is an arithmetic progression. With no fixed points, the terms usually repeat.

How do I show that a sequence given by a recurrence is monotonic and bounded?

If you know the general term, study the sign of the difference xₙ₊₁ − xₙ directly from the formula. Otherwise prove a bound by induction, for example that all the terms stay on the same side of a fixed point, then find the sign of xₙ₊₁ − xₙ from the relation. An increasing sequence is bounded below by its first term, and a decreasing one above.
