The question of the worksheet: How do you compute the limit of a sequence built from sums, products, quotients, powers and radicals, and what do you do when the rules of calculation give no answer?

Start from what you know

Three quick checks. If one of them gives you trouble, write it down: go back to it first.

  1. Fill in:

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}=\ldots$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n^2=\ldots$;

    c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n=\ldots$;

    d) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} 3^n=\ldots$;

    e) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(-1)^n=\ldots$.

  2. Factor out the dominant term: $3n^2-5n+2=n^2\left(\ldots\right)$ and $3^n+2^n=3^n\left(\ldots\right)$.

  3. Show that $\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}}$.

Theory in brief

First the rules of calculation and the cases where they do not apply. Then, for each type of sequence, the method step by step (in the thick frame) and worked examples. At the end you find the summary table and the logical thread.

1. Operations with convergent sequences

Theorem

If $a_n\to a$ and $b_n\to b$, with $a, b\in\mathbb{R}$, then:

  • sum and difference: $a_n\pm b_n\to a\pm b$;
  • product: $a_n\cdot b_n\to a\cdot b$; in particular $\lambda a_n\to\lambda a$, for $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$;
  • quotient: $\frac{a_n}{b_n}\to\frac{a}{b}$, if $b\ne 0$;
  • power: $a_n^{b_n}\to a^b$, if $a_n>0$ and $a>0$;
  • root: $\sqrt[k]{a_n}\to\sqrt[k]{a}$ (with $a_n\ge 0$, if $k$ is even);
  • absolute value: $|a_n|\to|a|$.

In short: the limit “passes through” the operations whenever the result makes sense. The rules are used without proof.

Example 1 · Convergent sequences

a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(3+\frac{2}{n}\right)\left(5-\frac{1}{n^2}\right)=3\cdot 5=15$

b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{4+\frac{1}{n}}=\sqrt{4}=2$

c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2+\frac{1}{n}}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2=\frac{1}{4}$ (the base and the exponent have finite limits, and the limit of the base is positive).

2. Possible calculations with ±∞

When at least one of the limits is infinite, the result is read from the table below. Here $a, b\in\mathbb{R}$ are finite limits, and $0_+$ ($0_-$) means the limit $0$, with positive (negative) terms from some rank on.

Operation Possible calculations
Addition $a+(+\infty)=+\infty,\quad a+(-\infty)=-\infty$
$(+\infty)+(+\infty)=+\infty,\quad(-\infty)+(-\infty)=-\infty$
Multiplication $a\cdot(+\infty)=+\infty$ and $a\cdot(-\infty)=-\infty$, if $a>0$
$a\cdot(+\infty)=-\infty$ and $a\cdot(-\infty)=+\infty$, if $a<0$
$(+\infty)\cdot(+\infty)=(-\infty)\cdot(-\infty)=+\infty,\quad(+\infty)\cdot(-\infty)=-\infty$
Division $\frac{a}{+\infty}=\frac{a}{-\infty}=0$
$\frac{+\infty}{a}=+\infty$, if $a>0$; $\frac{+\infty}{a}=-\infty$, if $a<0$
$\frac{a}{0_+}=+\infty$ and $\frac{a}{0_-}=-\infty$, if $a>0$ (the other way round, if $a<0$)
$\frac{+\infty}{0_+}=+\infty,\quad\frac{+\infty}{0_-}=-\infty$
Powers (positive base) $a^{+\infty}=+\infty$, if $a>1$; $a^{+\infty}=0$, if $0<a<1$
$a^{-\infty}=0$, if $a>1$; $a^{-\infty}=+\infty$, if $0<a<1$
$(+\infty)^b=+\infty$, if $b>0$; $(+\infty)^b=0$, if $b<0$
$(+\infty)^{+\infty}=+\infty,\quad(+\infty)^{-\infty}=0,\quad 0_+^{+\infty}=0,\quad 0_+^{-\infty}=+\infty$

$\sqrt[k]{-\infty}=-\infty$, for odd $k$

Remember

  • In a sum, a finite number “does not count” next to $\pm\infty$.
  • In products and quotients, the result follows the rule of signs.
  • For $\frac{a}{0}$, with $a\ne 0$, you must find the sign of the denominator: $\frac{1}{0}$ without a sign makes no sense.

Example 2 · Determinate forms

a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(n^2+5)=+\infty$ (the form $+\infty+5$).

b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{-3}{n+1}=0$ (the form $\frac{-3}{+\infty}$).

c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(2-n)\cdot 3^n=-\infty$ (the form $(-\infty)\cdot(+\infty)$).

d) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(2+\frac{1}{n}\right)^n=+\infty$ (the form $2^{+\infty}$), but $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n}\right)^n=0$ (the form $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{+\infty}$).

3. Indeterminate forms

Definition

An indeterminate form is a form for which the rules of calculation give no result. There are seven:

$$\infty-\infty,\quad 0\cdot\infty,\quad\frac{\infty}{\infty},\quad\frac{0}{0},\quad 1^{\infty},\quad\infty^{0},\quad 0^{0}$$

The result depends on the actual sequences. You transform the expression until you reach a form from the table in part 2.

The forms $1^{\infty}$, $\infty^{0}$ and $0^{0}$ appear in powers whose base and exponent both vary. They are solved with the number $e$.

Example 3 · Why ∞ − ∞ is not 0

All the differences below have the form $\infty-\infty$, but the results differ:

  • $(n+3)-n=3\to 3$
  • $n^2-n=n(n-1)\to+\infty$
  • $n-n^2=n(1-n)\to-\infty$
  • $\left(n+(-1)^n\right)-n=(-1)^n$, which has no limit.
Form Where it appears How to transform
$\infty-\infty$ polynomials; differences of roots; differences of fractions factor out the dominant term; multiply by the conjugate; bring to a common denominator
$\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ ratios of polynomials, roots or exponentials factor out the dominant term, top and bottom
$0\cdot\infty$ products write the product as a quotient, or multiply it out
$\frac{0}{0}$ quotients of sequences with limit 0 simplify; multiply by the conjugate; multiply top and bottom by a power of $n$
$1^{\infty}$, $\infty^{0}$, $0^{0}$ powers with varying base and exponent with the number $e$

Watch out · Common mistakes

  • $\infty-\infty$ is not $0$, $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ is not $1$, $0\cdot\infty$ is not $0$, and $1^{\infty}$ is not $1$.
  • On the other hand, $(+\infty)+(+\infty)$, $(+\infty)\cdot(+\infty)$, $(+\infty)^{+\infty}$ and $0_+^{+\infty}$ are not indeterminate forms.
  • Before any calculation, write down the form you have. If it is indeterminate, transform.

4. Basic limits

Remember

  • $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n^k=+\infty$ and $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^k}=0$, for $k\in\mathbb{N}^*$.
  • $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n^{\alpha}=+\infty$, if $\alpha>0$, and $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n^{\alpha}=0$, if $\alpha<0$. For example, $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}=+\infty$ and $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{n}}=0$.
  • The power sequence:

$$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} q^n=\begin{cases}+\infty, & q>1\\ 1, & q=1\\ 0, & q\in(-1;1)\\ \text{does not exist}, & q\le -1\end{cases}$$

  • $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{a}=1$, for $a>0$: the exponent $\frac{1}{n}$ tends to $0$, so $a^{\frac{1}{n}}\to a^0=1$.
  • If $(a_n)$ is bounded and $b_n\to 0$, then $a_nb_n\to 0$ (the comparison test).

Example 4 · The power sequence

a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n=0$, because $\frac{2}{3}\in(-1;1)$.

b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(-\frac{5}{4}\right)^n$ does not exist, because $-\frac{5}{4}\le -1$.

c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{0{,}9^n}=+\infty$: the denominator tends to $0_+$ (the form $\frac{1}{0_+}$).

5. Polynomials and ratios of polynomials

★ Method

Factoring out the dominant term

  1. I find the highest power of $n$ (the degree), in the numerator and in the denominator.
  2. I factor out this power: $3n^2-5n+2=n^2\left(3-\frac{5}{n}+\frac{2}{n^2}\right)$.
  3. In the bracket, every term except the first tends to 0. The bracket tends to the leading coefficient.
  4. I simplify (for a ratio) and read the result with the rules of calculation.

Remember

For $a_k\ne 0$ and $k\ge 1$: $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(a_kn^k+\ldots+a_1n+a_0)=a_k\cdot(+\infty)$, that is $+\infty$, if $a_k>0$, and $-\infty$, if $a_k<0$.

$$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_kn^k+\ldots+a_0}{b_pn^p+\ldots+b_0}=\begin{cases}0, & k<p\\ \frac{a_k}{b_p}, & k=p\\ \frac{a_k}{b_p}\cdot(+\infty), & k>p\end{cases}$$

In short: compare the degrees. For equal degrees, the limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients.

Example 5 · Polynomials

a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(2n^3-5n^2+1)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n^3\left(2-\frac{5}{n}+\frac{1}{n^3}\right)=(+\infty)\cdot 2=+\infty$

b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(3n-n^2)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n^2\left(\frac{3}{n}-1\right)=(+\infty)\cdot(-1)=-\infty$. The form was $\infty-\infty$.

Example 6 · Ratios of polynomials

a) Equal degrees:

$$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n^2-n+4}{5n^2+2}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2\left(3-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{4}{n^2}\right)}{n^2\left(5+\frac{2}{n^2}\right)}=\frac{3}{5}$$

b) The top degree is smaller: $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n+7}{2n^2+3}=0$.

c) The top degree is larger: $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-4n^3}{n^2+6n}=\frac{-4}{1}\cdot(+\infty)=-\infty$.

Watch out · The degree with brackets and roots

  • $(2n-1)^3$ has degree $3$ and leading coefficient $2^3=8$. There is no need to expand the bracket.
  • $\sqrt{4n^2+n}=n\sqrt{4+\frac{1}{n}}$ “behaves like” $2n$: it has degree $1$ and coefficient $2$. Likewise, $\sqrt[3]{8n^3+1}$ behaves like $2n$.
  • $\sqrt{n}$ has degree $\frac{1}{2}$, and $n\sqrt{n}$ has degree $\frac{3}{2}$.

Example 7 · A ratio with roots

$$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{4n^2+1}+n}{3n-2}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\left(\sqrt{4+\frac{1}{n^2}}+1\right)}{n\left(3-\frac{2}{n}\right)}=\frac{2+1}{3}=1$$

6. Exponentials

★ Method

Dividing by the dominant term

  1. I write all the powers with exponent $n$: $5^{n+1}=5\cdot 5^n$, $2^{2n}=4^n$, $3^{n-1}=\frac{3^n}{3}$.
  2. I choose the power with the largest base (in absolute value).
  3. I factor out this power, top and bottom.
  4. The ratios left have the form $q^n$, with $|q|<1$, so they tend to $0$.

Example 8 · A ratio of exponentials

$$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{2\cdot 5^n+3^n}{4\cdot 5^n-2^n}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{5^n\left(2+\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^n\right)}{5^n\left(4-\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^n\right)}=\frac{2+0}{4-0}=\frac{1}{2}$$

Example 9 · A difference of exponentials

The form $\infty-\infty$: $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(3^n-2^n)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} 3^n\left(1-\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n\right)=(+\infty)\cdot 1=+\infty$.

Watch out · Negative bases

$(-2)^n$ has no limit, but $\frac{(-2)^n}{3^n}=\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)^n\to 0$. With negative bases, compare the absolute values of the bases.

7. Roots: differences and the conjugate

★ Method

Multiplying by the conjugate

  1. I check whether it really is a “tight” $\infty-\infty$: I compare the leading coefficients ($\sqrt{n^2+3n}$ behaves like $n$).
  2. If the leading coefficients differ, I factor out $n$ and I am done.
  3. If they are equal, I multiply by the conjugate: $\sqrt{A}-B=\frac{A-B^2}{\sqrt{A}+B}$. On top, the dominant terms cancel.
  4. I get the form $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ and factor out $n$, top and bottom.

Remember · Conjugate formulas

$$\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B}=\frac{A-B}{\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}}$$

$$\sqrt{A}-B=\frac{A-B^2}{\sqrt{A}+B}$$

$$\sqrt[3]{A}-B=\frac{A-B^3}{\sqrt[3]{A^2}+B\sqrt[3]{A}+B^2}$$

Example 10 · Step by step

Compute $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n^2+4n}-n\right)$.

Steps 1–2. The form $\infty-\infty$; $\sqrt{n^2+4n}$ behaves like $n$: the coefficients are equal.

Step 3. $\sqrt{n^2+4n}-n=\frac{n^2+4n-n^2}{\sqrt{n^2+4n}+n}=\frac{4n}{\sqrt{n^2+4n}+n}$

Step 4. $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{4n}{n\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{4}{n}}+1\right)}=\frac{4}{1+1}=2$

Example 11 · Two roots

$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n+3}-\sqrt{n}\right)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{3}{\sqrt{n+3}+\sqrt{n}}=0$ (the form $\frac{3}{+\infty}$).

Example 12 · Different coefficients

$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n^2+3n}-2n\right)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} n\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{3}{n}}-2\right)=(+\infty)\cdot(-1)=-\infty$. No conjugate was needed.

8. Sums with n terms and bounded sequences

Watch out · How many terms does the sum have?

The rules of calculation are for a fixed number of terms. The sum $\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}+\ldots+\frac{1}{n}$, with $n$ terms, equals $1$, although each term tends to $0$. First compute the sum (write it without “…”), then the limit.

Remember · Useful sums

$$1+2+\ldots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

$$1+3+5+\ldots+(2n-1)=n^2$$

$$1^2+2^2+\ldots+n^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$$

$$1+q+q^2+\ldots+q^n=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1},\quad q\ne 1$$

$$\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}$$

Example 13 · A sum

$$\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1+2+\ldots+n}{n^2}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n(n+1)}{2n^2}=\frac{1}{2}$$

Example 14 · Bounded times a sequence with limit 0

a) $\left|\frac{\sin n}{n}\right|\le\frac{1}{n}\to 0$, so $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sin n}{n}=0$.

b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n+\cos n}{2n+1}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1+\frac{\cos n}{n}}{2+\frac{1}{n}}=\frac{1}{2}$, because $\frac{\cos n}{n}\to 0$.

Watch out · Both sequences must have a limit

The rules of calculation need both sequences to have a limit. The sequence $(\sin n)$ has no limit, so you cannot write $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sin n\cdot\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}$. Use the comparison test.

Summary table: what to do for each kind of sequence

What the sequence looks like Form What I do
a polynomial in $n$ $\infty-\infty$ factor out the highest power
a ratio of polynomials or roots $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ compare the degrees; factor out top and bottom
a ratio of exponentials $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ divide by the power with the largest base
a difference with roots, equal leading coefficients $\infty-\infty$ multiply by the conjugate
a difference with roots, different leading coefficients $\infty-\infty$ factor out $n$
a difference of fractions $\infty-\infty$ bring to a common denominator
a sum with $n$ terms — compute the sum first
bounded times a sequence with limit 0 — the comparison test

★ Most important

The logical thread: how to compute the limit of a sequence. Follow the steps in this order, in every problem

  1. I replace, in my head, each piece with its limit and write down the form I get.
  2. If the form is in the table of possible calculations, I write the result directly.
  3. If it is an indeterminate form, I recognise the type of sequence (the table above).
  4. I transform: factor out, divide by the dominant term, use the conjugate or compute the sum.
  5. I write the form again: now it must be determinate.
  6. I check the sign and, if I can, the value for a large $n$.

Problems by level

For each limit, first write the form (for example $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$), then the transformation you make.

  1. Decide whether the form is indeterminate. If it is not, write the result:

    e) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(1-2n)^3}{n^3+2}$;

    f) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2+5n}{3n+1}$;

    g) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}{2n^3}$.

  5. Compute:

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{3^n+2^n}{3^n-1}$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{5^{n+1}+4^n}{5^n+3^n}$;

    c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{2^n+3^n}{4^n+1}$;

    f) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(5^n-4^n)$;

    g) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{6^n}{2^n+3^n}$.

  6. Compute:

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n^2+6n}-n\right)$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{4n^2+n}-2n\right)$;

    c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n+5}-\sqrt{n+1}\right)$;

    f) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(n-\sqrt{n^2-2n}\right)$.

Consolidation level

  1. First compute the sum, then the limit:

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1+2+\ldots+n}{n^2+1}$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1+3+5+\ldots+(2n-1)}{2n^2+n}$;

    c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1^2+2^2+\ldots+n^2}{n^3}$;

    d) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\ldots+\frac{1}{2^n}\right)$;

    e) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1\cdot 2}+\frac{1}{2\cdot 3}+\ldots+\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\right)$;

    f) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1+2+\ldots+n}{n+2}-\frac{n}{2}\right)$.

  2. Compute (compare the degrees):

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{9n^2+n}+2n}{n+5}$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n}+1}{\sqrt{4n+3}}$;

    c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt[3]{8n^3+n}-n}{2n+1}$;

    d) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\sqrt{n}+1}{2n^2+1}$;

    e) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n^2+1}+\sqrt{4n^2+3}}{\sqrt{n^2+5}}$;

    f) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n^4+n}}{n+1}$.

  3. Compute, using the comparison test where needed:

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}}$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\cos n}{n^2+1}$;

    c) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n+\sin n}{n+2}$;

    d) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{2^n+(-1)^n}{2^n}$.

  4. Parameters.

    a) Find $a\in\mathbb{R}$ for which $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{an^2+3n+1}{2n^2+5}=3$.

    b) Find $a\in\mathbb{R}$ for which the limit $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{(a-1)n^3+n}{n^2+1}$ is finite.

    c) Compute $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{an^2+n}{n+1}$, discussing the cases of $a\in\mathbb{R}$.

  5. Compute, discussing the cases of $a>0$:

    a) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{a^n+2^n}{3^n+2^n}$;

    b) $\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{a^{n+1}+2^n}{a^n+2^{n+1}}$.

  6. True or false? For false, give a counterexample.

    a) If $a_n\to+\infty$ and $b_n\to+\infty$, then $a_n-b_n\to 0$.

    b) If $a_n\to 0$ and $(b_n)$ is bounded, then $a_nb_n\to 0$.

    c) If the sequence $(a_n+b_n)$ has a limit, then the sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ have limits.

    d) If $a_n\to 2$ and $b_n\to-\infty$, then $a_nb_n\to-\infty$.

    e) If $a_n>0$, $a_n\to 1$ and $b_n\to+\infty$, then $a_n^{b_n}\to 1$.

    f) If $a_n>0$ and $a_n\to 0$, then $\frac{1}{a_n}\to+\infty$.

  7. Let $x_1=1$, $x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}x_n+3$, $n\ge 1$.

Frequently asked questions

What are the indeterminate forms for limits of sequences?

There are seven: ∞ − ∞, 0 · ∞, ∞/∞, 0/0, 1^∞, ∞^0 and 0^0. For them the rules of calculation give no result: the same form can lead to different limits. You transform the expression (factor out the dominant term, use the conjugate, divide by the dominant term) until you reach a determinate form.

How do I compute the limit of a ratio of polynomials in n?

Compare the degrees of the numerator and the denominator. If the top degree is smaller, the limit is 0. For equal degrees, the limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients. If the top degree is larger, the limit is +∞ or −∞, according to the sign of the ratio of the leading coefficients.

When do I multiply by the conjugate in a limit of a sequence?

When you have a difference with roots of the form ∞ − ∞ whose dominant terms have the same coefficient, for example √(n² + 4n) − n. The conjugate turns the difference into a quotient in which the dominant terms cancel. If the coefficients differ, factoring out the dominant term is enough.

Why can't I compute the limit of a sum with n terms term by term?

The rules of calculation are for a fixed number of terms. When the number of terms grows with n, you first compute the sum with a formula (Gauss's sum, the geometric progression, telescoping), then the limit. For example, 1/n added n times gives 1, although each term tends to 0.
