Worksheet: sequences, an introduction

Summary

The worksheet shows what a sequence is and how it can be given: in words, by listing terms, by a formula, by a recurrence or by a graph. Then come the sequence of partial sums, the general term for recurrences with a sum or a product, and monotonicity, studied with the difference, the ratio or by induction. Problems on three levels close it.

By the end of this worksheet you will be able to:

  • recognise a sequence and compute its terms;
  • move from one way of defining a sequence to another;
  • work with the sequence of partial sums;
  • find the general term for recurrences of the form $x_n=x_{n-1}+f(n)$ and $x_n=x_{n-1}\cdot f(n)$;
  • decide whether a sequence is monotonic using the difference, the ratio or induction.

1. What is a sequence

Definition

A sequence of real numbers is a function $f:\mathbb{N}^*\to \mathbb{R}$. We write $a_n=f(n)$.

  • $n$ is the index (rank) of the term.
  • $a_n$ is the term of index $n$, also called the general term.
  • We write the sequence as $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$ or, briefly, $a_1;\ a_2;\ a_3;\ \ldots$

The index $n$ is always a natural number. The term $a_n$ can be any real number.

Sometimes the numbering starts at $0$: then we write $(a_n)_{n\ge 0}$.

Careful! The order of the terms matters. The sequences $1;\ 2;\ 3;\ 4;\ \ldots$ and $2;\ 1;\ 4;\ 3;\ \ldots$ are different.

2. Ways to define a sequence

The same sequence can be given in several ways. Here is the sequence $3;\ 9;\ 19;\ 33;\ 51;\ \ldots$ written in five ways:

Way What it looks like What it is good for
In words (descriptive) “each term is 1 more than twice the square of its index” states the rule plainly
By listing the first terms $3;\ 9;\ 19;\ 33;\ 51;\ \ldots$ shows how the sequence “starts”
By a general-term formula $a_n=2n^2+1$, $n\ge 1$ gives any term directly: $a_{10}=201$
By a recurrence relation $a_1=3$, $a_n=a_{n-1}+4n-2$, $n\ge 2$ each term comes from the one before
By a table or a graph the points $(1;3)$, $(2;9)$, $(3;19)$, … shows how the sequence changes

Remember

  • A recurrence relation must also give the first term. Without it, the sequence is not well defined.
  • The graph of a sequence is made of isolated points $(n;\ a_n)$. We do not join them with a line.
  • A few terms alone do not determine the sequence. From a list we find a possible formula and check it on the given terms.

Example 1

Let $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$ be the sequence $a_n=\frac{n+4}{n+2}$.

a) The first four terms: $a_1=\frac{5}{3}$, $a_2=\frac{6}{4}=\frac{3}{2}$, $a_3=\frac{7}{5}$, $a_4=\frac{8}{6}=\frac{4}{3}$.

b) Is $\frac{13}{12}$ a term of the sequence? We solve $\frac{n+4}{n+2}=\frac{13}{12}$: $12n+48=13n+26$, so $n=22$. Yes, $\frac{13}{12}=a_{22}$.

c) The graph: the points $(n;\ a_n)$ for $n=1;\ 2;\ \ldots;\ 8$.

3. The sequence of partial sums

Definition

For a sequence $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$ we write

$$S_n=a_1+a_2+\ldots+a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{a_k}.$$

The sequence $(S_n)_{n\ge 1}$ is called the sequence of partial sums.

  • $S_1=a_1$ and $S_n=S_{n-1}+a_n$, for $n\ge 2$.
  • Conversely, if we know $S_n$: $a_1=S_1$ and $a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}$, for $n\ge 2$.

Careful! The formula $a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}$ does not apply for $n=1$. We check separately whether the formula we found also gives the term $a_1$.

Example 2

The sum of the first $n$ terms of a sequence is $S_n=n^2+3n$. We find $a_n$.

  • $a_1=S_1=1+3=4$.
  • For $n\ge 2$: $a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}=n^2+3n-(n-1)^2-3(n-1)=2n+2$.
  • For $n=1$ the formula gives $2\cdot 1+2=4=a_1$. So $a_n=2n+2$ for every $n\ge 1$.

4. The general term from recurrence relations

4.1. Recurrences of the form $x_n=x_{n-1}+f(n)$

★ Method

Add the relations

  1. Write the relation for $n=2;\ 3;\ \ldots;\ n$: $x_2=x_1+f(2)$, $x_3=x_2+f(3)$, …, $x_n=x_{n-1}+f(n)$.
  2. Add the relations side by side. The terms $x_2;\ x_3;\ \ldots;\ x_{n-1}$ appear on both sides and cancel.
  3. What is left is $x_n=x_1+f(2)+f(3)+\ldots+f(n)$. Compute the sum with the known sums.
  4. Check the formula on $x_1$ and $x_2$.

Example 3

$x_1=1$ and $x_n=x_{n-1}+6n-5$, for $n\ge 2$.

Adding the relations: $x_n=1+\sum_{k=2}^{n}{(6k-5)}$. For $k=1$ the term $6k-5$ is exactly $1=x_1$, so

$$x_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{(6k-5)}=6\cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2}-5n=3n^2-2n.$$

Check: $x_1=3-2=1$ and $x_2=1+7=8=12-4$. ✓

4.2. Recurrences of the form $x_n=x_{n-1}\cdot f(n)$

★ Method

Multiply the relations

  1. Write the relation for $n=2;\ 3;\ \ldots;\ n$: $x_2=x_1\cdot f(2)$, $x_3=x_2\cdot f(3)$, …, $x_n=x_{n-1}\cdot f(n)$.
  2. Multiply the relations side by side. Cancel $x_2;\ x_3;\ \ldots;\ x_{n-1}$ (they are nonzero).
  3. What is left is $x_n=x_1\cdot f(2)\cdot f(3)\cdot \ldots\cdot f(n)$. The fractions cancel “in a chain”.
  4. Check the formula on $x_1$ and $x_2$.

Example 4

$x_1=1$ and $x_n=\frac{2n}{n+1}\cdot x_{n-1}$, for $n\ge 2$.

$$x_n=1\cdot \frac{2\cdot 2}{3}\cdot \frac{2\cdot 3}{4}\cdot \frac{2\cdot 4}{5}\cdot \ldots\cdot \frac{2\cdot n}{n+1}.$$

The factor $2$ appears $n-1$ times. The fractions $\frac{2}{3}\cdot \frac{3}{4}\cdot \ldots\cdot \frac{n}{n+1}$ cancel “in a chain” and leave $\frac{2}{n+1}$. So

$$x_n=2^{n-1}\cdot \frac{2}{n+1}=\frac{2^n}{n+1}.$$

Check: $x_1=\frac{2}{2}=1$ and $x_2=\frac{4}{3}\cdot 1=\frac{2^2}{3}$. ✓

5. Monotonic sequences

Definition

The sequence $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$ is:

  • strictly increasing if $a_n<a_{n+1}$ for every $n\ge 1$;
  • increasing if $a_n\le a_{n+1}$ for every $n\ge 1$;
  • strictly decreasing if $a_n>a_{n+1}$ for every $n\ge 1$;
  • decreasing if $a_n\ge a_{n+1}$ for every $n\ge 1$.

An increasing or decreasing sequence is called monotonic.

This is the monotonicity of the function $f:\mathbb{N}^*\to \mathbb{R}$, $f(n)=a_n$. For sequences it is enough to compare two consecutive terms.

A sequence is not monotonic if we find three terms that “go up and then down” (or the other way round). For example, $a_1<a_2$ and $a_2>a_3$.

Remember · Three methods

1. The difference. Compute $a_{n+1}-a_n$, simplify it and study its sign. If $a_{n+1}-a_n>0$ for every $n$, the sequence is strictly increasing. If $a_{n+1}-a_n<0$ for every $n$, the sequence is strictly decreasing.

2. The ratio. Use it only if all terms are strictly positive. Compare $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}$ with $1$. If the ratio is greater than $1$, the sequence is strictly increasing. If it is less than $1$, the sequence is strictly decreasing. The method suits terms with powers or products.

3. Induction. Use it when the sequence is given by a recurrence $x_{n+1}=g(x_n)$. Check $x_1<x_2$ (or $x_1>x_2$). Show that $x_k<x_{k+1}$ implies $x_{k+1}<x_{k+2}$.

Example 5

a) The difference. $a_n=\frac{4n+1}{n+2}$.

$$a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{4n+5}{n+3}-\frac{4n+1}{n+2}=\frac{(4n+5)(n+2)-(4n+1)(n+3)}{(n+2)(n+3)}=\frac{7}{(n+2)(n+3)}>0.$$

The sequence is strictly increasing.

b) The ratio. The sequence from Example 4: $x_n=\frac{2^n}{n+1}$. All terms are strictly positive.

$$\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}=\frac{2^{n+1}}{n+2}\cdot \frac{n+1}{2^n}=\frac{2n+2}{n+2}>1,$$

because $2n+2>n+2$ for every $n\ge 1$. The sequence is strictly increasing.

c) Induction. $x_1=1$ and $x_{n+1}=\frac{x_n+6}{3}$, for $n\ge 1$. We show that $x_n<x_{n+1}$ for every $n\ge 1$.

  • Check: $x_2=\frac{7}{3}>1=x_1$.
  • Inductive step: assume $x_k<x_{k+1}$. Then $x_k+6<x_{k+1}+6$, so $\frac{x_k+6}{3}<\frac{x_{k+1}+6}{3}$, that is $x_{k+1}<x_{k+2}$.

By the principle of mathematical induction, the sequence is strictly increasing.

Exercises

Basic level

  1. Consider the sequence $(a_n)_{n\ge 1}$, $a_n=\frac{2n-1}{n+2}$.

    a) Compute $a_1$, $a_2$, $a_3$ and $a_4$.

    b) Show that $\frac{13}{9}$ is a term of the sequence and find its index.

    c) Is the number $2$ a term of the sequence? Justify.

  2. Write the first five terms of each sequence:

    a) the sequence of prime numbers, in increasing order;

    b) the sequence of nonzero perfect squares, in increasing order;

    c) the sequence $(r_n)_{n\ge 1}$, where $r_n$ is the remainder of $n$ divided by $3$.

  3. Find a possible formula for the general term, $n\ge 1$:

    a) $3;\ 5;\ 7;\ 9;\ \ldots$

    b) $\frac{1}{2};\ \frac{2}{3};\ \frac{3}{4};\ \frac{4}{5};\ \ldots$

    c) $-1;\ 1;\ -1;\ 1;\ \ldots$

    d) $0;\ 3;\ 8;\ 15;\ 24;\ \ldots$

    e) $\frac{1}{3};\ \frac{1}{9};\ \frac{1}{27};\ \frac{1}{81};\ \ldots$

  4. Recurrence relations.

    a) The sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge 1}$ has $x_1=2$ and $x_{n+1}=2x_n-1$, for $n\ge 1$. Compute $x_2$, $x_3$, $x_4$ and $x_5$.

    b) The first terms of a sequence are $1;\ 4;\ 13;\ 40;\ 121$. Find a recurrence relation of the form $x_{n+1}=a\cdot x_n+b$ that generates them.

    Hint: compare each term with three times the one before it.

  5. Let $a_n=(-1)^n\cdot (2n-1)$, $n\ge 1$. Write the first five terms, then compute $S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$, $S_4$ and $S_5$.

  6. Study the monotonicity of the sequences using the difference:

    a) $a_n=5-\frac{2}{n}$;

    b) $b_n=\frac{n+4}{n+1}$.

Intermediate level

  1. Find the general term $a_n$ of the sequence whose sum of the first $n$ terms is:

    a) $S_n=3n^2-n$;

    b) $S_n=n^2+n+1$.

    Hint: in b) compare the formula you get for $n\ge 2$ with $a_1$.

  2. Find the general term of the sequence:

    a) $x_1=2$, $x_n=x_{n-1}+6n-2$, for $n\ge 2$;

    b) $x_1=4$, $x_n=x_{n-1}+3n^2+1$, for $n\ge 2$.

    Hint: use the sums $\sum_{k=1}^{n}{k}$ and $\sum_{k=1}^{n}{k^2}$.

  3. Find the general term of the sequence:

    a) $x_1=1$, $x_n=\frac{n+2}{n-1}\cdot x_{n-1}$, for $n\ge 2$;

    b) $x_1=4$, $x_n=\frac{n-1}{n+1}\cdot x_{n-1}$, for $n\ge 2$.

  4. Study the monotonicity of the sequences using the ratio:

    a) $a_n=\frac{n}{3^n}$;

    b) $b_n=\frac{2\cdot 5\cdot 8\cdot \ldots\cdot (3n-1)}{3\cdot 6\cdot 9\cdot \ldots\cdot (3n)}$.

  5. A video has $10$ views on its first day online. On each following day, the number of views on day $n$ is $2n$ more than on the day before.

    a) Let $x_n$ be the number of views on day $n$. Write the recurrence relation.

    b) Find the general term $x_n$. How many views does the video get on day $10$?

    c) How many views does the video get in total over the first $10$ days?

  6. The sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge 1}$ has $x_1=5$ and $x_{n+1}=\frac{x_n+3}{4}$, for $n\ge 1$. Show by induction that the sequence is strictly decreasing.

Advanced level

  1. The sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge 1}$ has $x_1=1$ and $x_n=\frac{n(n+1)}{(n-1)(n+2)}\cdot x_{n-1}$, for $n\ge 2$.

    a) Find the general term.

    b) Study the monotonicity of the sequence.

    Hint: split the product into two “chains”: $\frac{2}{1}\cdot \frac{3}{2}\cdot \ldots\cdot \frac{n}{n-1}$ and $\frac{3}{4}\cdot \frac{4}{5}\cdot \ldots\cdot \frac{n+1}{n+2}$.

  2. Let $a_n=\frac{n^2}{2^n}$, $n\ge 1$.

    a) Show that $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}<1$ if and only if $n^2-2n-1>0$.

    b) Compute $a_1$, $a_2$, $a_3$, $a_4$. Is the sequence monotonic? From which index on do the terms decrease?

  3. The sequence $(x_n)_{n\ge 1}$ has $x_1=1$ and $x_{n+1}=\sqrt{x_n+2}$, for $n\ge 1$.

    a) Show by induction that $x_n<2$ for every $n\ge 1$.

    b) Show by induction that the sequence is strictly increasing.

  4. Back to the sequence from exercise 4 a): $x_1=2$, $x_{n+1}=2x_n-1$.

    a) Compare the terms $x_1;\ \ldots;\ x_5$ with the powers of $2$. Guess a formula for $x_n$.

    b) Prove the formula by mathematical induction.

For those who want more

  1. Find the real numbers $m$ for which the sequence $a_n=\frac{n+m}{2^n}$, $n\ge 1$, is strictly decreasing. What happens for $m=0$?

    Hint: use the difference. Why can we not use the ratio for every $m$?

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a general-term formula and a recurrence relation?

A general-term formula gives any term directly: you substitute n and compute. A recurrence relation gives a term from the ones before it, so you need the first term and you compute step by step. For recurrences of the form xₙ = xₙ₋₁ + f(n) or xₙ = xₙ₋₁ · f(n) you can find the general term by adding, or multiplying, the relations.

How do I find the general term of a sequence when I know the sum of its first n terms?

You use the sequence of partial sums: a₁ = S₁ and aₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁ for n ≥ 2. The second formula does not work for n = 1, so you check separately whether the formula you found also gives the first term. If it does not, you write the general term in two cases.

When do I use the difference and when the ratio to study the monotonicity of a sequence?

The difference aₙ₊₁ − aₙ works for any sequence and suits fractions and sums: you study its sign. The ratio aₙ₊₁ / aₙ, compared with 1, is used only when all terms are strictly positive and is handy for powers and products. When the sequence is given only by a recurrence, monotonicity is usually shown by induction.
